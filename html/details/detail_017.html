YouTube揭示了哪些人工智能视频无法赚钱

美国2026-07-21T07:00:00生成于 2026-08-14 11:28

YouTube 多年来一直鼓励创作者尝试人工智能，同时试图限制这些工具产生的内容类型。现在，关于什么越界的问题变得更加具体。

在该公司 7 月 16 日的 Inside YouTube 播客中，信任与安全副总裁 Matt Halprin 与创作者内部人士 Rene Ritchie 坐下来讨论 YouTube 如何区分使用人工智能作为制作工具的创作者和使用人工智能大规模制作视频的频道。

这次谈话并不是宣布 YouTube 将禁止人工智能生成的内容；而是宣布 YouTube 将禁止人工智能生成的内容。相反，它仔细研究了 3 种可能使频道失去赚钱资格的视频。

哪些人工智能视频会失去变现能力？

第一类：通用或重复的视频，每次上传之间几乎没有什么区别。这包括播出人工智能生成的故事的频道，其中只有角色名称、设置或随附图像发生变化。它还涵盖幻灯片、滚动文本、动画或教程，这些内容重复了相同的基本信息，但不提供任何新内容。

这并不意味着创作者需要在每次上传时彻底改造他们的频道。重要的是内容：每个视频都应该讲述一个新的故事、教训、论点或解释。它不应该提供已发布内容的经过轻微编辑的版本。

在没有机上创作者的情况下制作视频的市场是巨大的。 Snap 生成人工智能的前负责人亚历克斯·马什拉博夫 (Alex Mashrabov) 在 6 月份接受《好莱坞报道》采访时声称，人工智能生成的不露面视频是“一个新兴的类别，个体创业者和讲故事的人可以蓬勃发展”。现在他们必须在不重复的情况下蓬勃发展。

第二类：YouTube 所说的“不满意或令人反感”的内容。这些视频严重依赖令人不安的图像或情绪操纵来吸引注意力，但并没有围绕它构建太多故事。

YouTube 的 Halprin 讨论了一个例子：视频显示动物明显处于危险之中，然后有人突然扑救它们。无论视频是否是人工智能生成的，围绕这一概念建立的频道都可能会失去盈利能力。

同样的原则可能适用于令人不安的剪辑、以暴力为重点的重复视频的汇编，或者错误地显示名人死亡或自然灾害发生的人工智能镜头。

再次强调，YouTube 并不是说​​所有涉及悲剧、暴力或痛苦的视频都不能赚钱。新闻报道、纪录片、虚构连续剧和其他实质性作品仍然可以涵盖这些主题。问题是这些令人不安的材料是否有更大的故事意义，或者只是被用作标题诱饵。

第三类与生成人工智能直接相关。 YouTube 表示，频道不能通过合成人物角色在医疗保健、金融、法律和政治等敏感话题上展现人类权威的视频来获利。

这可能包括人工智能生成的“医生”诊断症状、人工智能生成的播客主持人推荐投资，或者人工智能生成的“律师”为观众提供有关法庭案件的建议。令人担忧的不是这些角色是虚构的，而是观众可能会误认为他们是可信的人类专家。

经过复杂的镇压之后才做出澄清

YouTube 多年来一直在努力限制重复内容。 2025 年，该公司将此类内容重新命名为“不真实”，并指出重复和大规模制作的视频以前没有资格获利。

生成式人工智能使这种材料的制作变得更加容易。 “人工智能实际上可以让人们制作大量视频，”哈尔普林指出，同时承认增加的产出可以包括真正好的作品。

“但是，完全相同的新工具可以让你快速制作大量非常相似的视频，”他补充道。 “它们非常通用，没有真正的叙事弧线，也没有真正展示你的创造力。”

这种产出的规模正变得难以忽视。

在 2025 年的一项研究中，视频编辑公司 Kapwing 创建了一个全新的 YouTube 帐户，并分析了该平台推荐的前 500 个视频。研究人员将其中 104 项（约占 21%）归为“AI 垃圾”。第三个属于更广泛的类别“脑腐”，其中包括旨在吸引观众注意力的其他形式的低质量内容。

Kapwing 检查了 15,000 个热门频道，发现 278 个频道仅包含人工智能生成的内容。这些频道的观看次数和订阅人数合计超过 630 亿次和 2.21 亿人。该公司估计他们的年收入约为 1.17 亿美元。

YouTube 表示，调查结果并不一定反映整个平台；然而，该公司终止了研究中确定的一个渠道，并从其货币化计划中删除了其他几个渠道。

首席执行官 Neal Mohan 将管理 AI 问题描述为 YouTube 2026 年的首要任务之一，尽管该公司推出了自己的 AI 工具。

YouTube 将 Google DeepMind 的 Veo 视频生成器直接嵌入到 Shorts 中，并提供了一些工具，可以对照片进行动画处理、组合原始素材、提出想法以及自动将视频配音成 27 种语言。 12 月份每天都有超过 100 万个频道使用这些人工智能创作工具。

那么，莫汉的平台正处于一个棘手的境地。它希望这项技术能够扩大创作者的创作范围，同时又不让其收入分享计划成为自动化内容农场的发薪日。

为什么“不露面”创作者会担心

此次打击行动让一些创作原创视频但不出现在镜头前的创作者感到担忧。

这些频道通常被描述为“不露面”，这一类别包括叙述纪录片、历史视频、动画、解说、真实犯罪、环境声音和其他教育作品。

许多技术早在生成式人工智能出现之前就已经存在，并且依赖于幕后的人类作家、研究人员、编辑、插画家、动画师和配音演员。

问题是，人工智能内容农场表面上看起来往往很相似。一些创作者表示，相似性已经造成了问题。

NOS 博士是一位拥有 180 万订阅者的教育 YouTuber，他告诉《THR》创作者，制作类似内容而不出现在镜头上的内容将被取消货币化。据报道，其他不露面的频道运营商已经开始通过 Fiverr 和 Upwork 等演出平台招聘视频主持人，希望屏幕上的人能让他们的视频更加人造。

这些故事仍然是轶事，而且此类举措在技术上是不必要的。 YouTube 并未表示出现在镜头前可以提高频道赚钱的机会，而且其书面政策也不要求创作者露面。尽管如此，不露面的创作者仍然没有一种简单的方法来展示屏幕后面的人类工作。

YouTube并未整体禁止人工智能视频

人工智能本身并没有取消资格。它已经可以执行制作 YouTube 视频所涉及的许多单独任务，包括起草脚本、生成图像和声音、创建动画、翻译语音和构建视觉效果。 YouTube 甚至为创作者提供了自己的人工智能制作和配音工具。

YouTube 的 Halprin 表示，该公司不会仅仅根据是否涉及生成人工智能来判断视频。创作者可以在整个制作过程中使用人工智能，并且如果完成的作品包含原创故事、有用信息、娱乐或其他明确的创意贡献，则仍然有资格获得货币化。

问题在于，使用这些相同的工具可以更轻松地制作大量看起来、声音和感觉几乎相同的视频。

哈尔普林说：“人工智能实际上可以让人们制作大量视频。”他指出，这项技术可以增加创作者创作的真正优秀作品的数量。

“但是，完全相同的新工具可以让你快速制作大量非常相似的视频，”他补充道。 “它们非常通用，没有真正的叙事弧线，也没有真正展示你的创造力。”

YouTube 更新后的指南旨在向创作者展示这条模糊线的起点。

当 YouTube 取消某个频道的货币化功能时会发生什么？

在决定某个频道是否符合 YouTube 合作伙伴计划的资格时，YouTube 可能会审核多个视频。

审核者可以考虑频道的主要主题、最新上传的内容、观看次数最多的视频、观看时间的最大来源、标题、缩略图、描述和“关于”页面。如果频道的很大一部分属于三个不真实内容类别之一，YouTube 可以从整个频道中删除获利功能。

Halprin 还担心，愤怒的观众可能会因为重复举报该频道而将创作者从 YPP 中删除。这位高管表示，举报可以促使 YouTube 更仔细地审查某个频道，但仅凭举报数量并不能决定该频道是否会失去盈利能力。

从该计划中删除的创作者通常有 21 天的时间提出上诉。如果上诉失败，他们可以修改频道、发布新材料，并在 90 天后重新申请。对于观众来说，澄清并不意味着 AI 垃圾会突然从 YouTube 上消失。

违反货币化标准的视频可能仍然可用，而且平台的规模使得自动化内容难以完全消除。 YouTube 能做的就是降低这些材料的利润。

英文原文（全文）

YouTube has spent years encouraging creators to experiment with AI while trying to contain the kind of content those tools produce. Now it’s getting more specific about what crosses the line. In a July 16 episode of the company’s Inside YouTube podcast, Vice President of Trust and Safety Matt Halprin sits down with creator insider ‪Rene Ritchie how YouTube distinguishes between creators using AI as a production tool and channels that use it to manufacture videos at scale. The conversation wasn't an announcement that YouTube is banning AI-generated content; instead, it offered a closer look at 3 types of videos that can make a channel ineligible to earn money. The first category: generic or repetitive videos that barely differ from one upload to the next. This includes channels pumping out AI-generated stories where only the character names, settings, or accompanying images change. It also covers slideshows, scrolling text, animations, or tutorials that rehash the same basic information without offering anything new. This doesn't mean creators need to completely reinvent their channels with every upload. What matters is the substance: each video should deliver a new story, lesson, argument, or explanation. It shouldn't serve up a lightly edited version of something already published. The market for producing videos without an on-camera creator is substantial. Alex Mashrabov, a former head of generative AI at Snap, claimed AI-generated faceless videos were "a new, emerging category where solopreneurs and storytellers can thrive" in a June interview with The Hollywood Reporter. Now they'll have to thrive without repetition. The second category: what YouTube calls “unsatisfying or off-putting” content. These videos lean heavily on disturbing imagery or emotional manipulation to grab attention, but don’t build much of a story around it. One example YouTube's Halprin discussed: videos that show animals in apparent danger before someone swoops in to rescue them. A channel built around this concept could lose monetization regardless of whether the footage was AI-generated. The same principle may apply to compilations of disturbing clips, repetitive videos focused on violence, or AI footage that falsely shows a celebrity dying or a natural disaster unfolding. Again, YouTube isn't saying that every video involving tragedy, violence, or distress can't earn money. News reports, documentaries, fictional series, and other substantive work can still cover these subjects. The question is whether the disturbing material serves a larger story, or is just being used as clickbait. The third category ties directly to generative AI. YouTube says channels can't monetize videos where synthetic personas present themselves as human authorities on sensitive topics such as healthcare, finance, law, and politics. This could include an AI-generated "doctor" diagnosing symptoms, an AI-generated podcast host recommending investments, or an AI-generated "lawyer" advising viewers on court cases. The concern isn't that the characters in question are fictional — it's that viewers might mistake them for credible human experts. YouTube has been trying to limit repetitive content for years. In 2025, the company rebranded such content as "inauthentic," noting that repetitive and mass-produced videos were previously ineligible for monetization. Generative AI has made producing that material considerably easier. “AI can actually allow people to make a lot of videos,” Halprin noted, while conceding that the increased output can include genuinely good work. "But that exact same new tool can allow you to make lots of videos really quickly that are very similar," he added. “They’re very generic and don’t really have a narrative arc and don’t really show your creativity.” The scale of that output is becoming difficult to ignore. In a 2025 study, video-editing company Kapwing created a brand new YouTube account and analyzed the first 500 videos the platform recommended. Researchers classified 104 of them—about 21 percent—as “AI slop.” A third fell into a broader category, “brainrot,” which includes other forms of low-quality content designed to capture viewers’ attention. Kapwing examined 15,000 popular channels and identified 278 that contained only AI-generated content. Together, these channels racked up over 63 billion views and 221 million subscribers. The company estimated they generate around $117 million in annual revenue. YouTube said the findings didn't necessarily reflect the platform as a whole; however, the company terminated one channel identified in the study and removed several others from its monetization programs. CEO Neal Mohan described managing AI slop as one of YouTube’s top priorities for 2026, even as the company rolls out its own AI tools. YouTube is embedding Google DeepMind’s Veo video generator directly into Shorts and offering tools that animate photos, assemble raw footage, suggest ideas, and automatically dub videos into 27 languages. More than 1 million channels used these AI creation tools each day in December. Mohan's platform is in a tricky position, then. It wants this technology to expand what creators can make—without letting its revenue-sharing program become a payday for automated content farms. The crackdown has worried some creators who make original videos without appearing on camera. These channels are often described as “faceless,” a category that includes narrated documentaries, history videos, animation, explainers, true crime, ambient sound, and other educational work. Many existed long before generative AI and rely on human writers, researchers, editors, illustrators, animators, and voice actors behind the scenes. Trouble is, AI content farms often look similar on the surface. And some creators say that resemblance is already creating problems. Doctor NOS, an educational YouTuber with 1.8 million subscribers, told THR creators making similar content without appearing on camera were being demonetized. Other faceless-channel operators have reportedly begun hiring on-camera hosts, via gig platforms like Fiverr and Upwork, in the hopes that a person on screen will make their videos more obviously human-made. Those stories remain anecdotal, and such moves are technically unnecessary. YouTube has not said that appearing on camera improves a channel’s chances of earning money, and its written policy does not require creators to show their faces. Still, faceless creators are left without an easy way to demonstrate human work behind the screen. AI itself is not disqualifying. It can already perform many of the individual tasks involved in making a YouTube video, including drafting scripts, generating images and voices, creating animation, translating speech, and building visual effects. YouTube even offers creators its own AI-powered production and dubbing tools. YouTube's Halprin said the company is not judging a video solely by whether generative AI was involved. A creator can use AI throughout the production process and remain eligible for monetization if the finished work contains an original story, useful information, entertainment, or another clear creative contribution. The problem is that those same tools make it much easier to produce large numbers of videos that look, sound, and feel almost identical. “AI can actually allow people to make a lot of videos,” Halprin said, noting that the technology can increase the amount of genuinely good work a creator produces. “But that exact same new tool can allow you to make lots of videos really quickly that are very similar,” he added. “They’re very generic and don’t really have a narrative arc and don’t really show your creativity.” YouTube’s updated guidance is intended to show creators where that fuzzy line begins. When deciding whether a channel qualifies for YPP, YouTube may review more than one individual video. Reviewers can consider the channel’s primary subject, newest uploads, most-viewed videos, largest sources of watch time, titles, thumbnails, descriptions, and About page. If a significant portion of the channel falls into one of the three inauthentic-content categories, YouTube can remove monetization from the entire channel. Halprin also addressed fears that angry viewers could get a creator removed from YPP simply by reporting the channel repeatedly. Reports can prompt YouTube to examine a channel more closely, the executive said, but the number of reports alone does not determine whether it loses monetization. Creators removed from the program generally have 21 days to appeal. If that appeal fails, they can revise the channel, publish new material, and reapply after 90 days. For viewers, the clarification does not mean AI slop will suddenly disappear from YouTube. Videos that violate monetization standards may remain available, and the scale of the platform makes automated content difficult to eliminate entirely. What YouTube can do is make that material less lucrative.
